Where the Swarm Pays

Seven model families, 240 hard problems, every cheap way of multiplying agents: voting, resampling, debate, roles, synthesis. On the answer the group does not beat its best member once a cut-off answer is counted as what it is. What it adds is a comparator, a second chance for a model that ran out of tokens, and a lesson about gold labels.

Abstract Three earlier notes argued that a group of LLM agents is bounded by its effective number of independent voters, and that debate raises that correlation rather than escaping it. This note is the ledger. We built one corpus of answer traces so that most of the questions the agent-swarm literature asks could be put to the same data: seven reasoning models (Claude Opus 5.5, Sonnet 5.5, Haiku 4.5, DeepSeek V3.2, GLM 5.3, Qwen 3.7, Mistral Large 2512) each answer 240 problems from MMLU-Pro, GPQA Diamond, GSM-Hard and the 2024–25 AIME; three of them draw 8–16 answers each at temperature 1; six of them debate for three rounds with every turn stored; DeepSeek runs the same debate as six copies of itself, with and without seeing its peers, and under four role prompts; five of them judge each other's candidates, first by picking one from a list and then by certifying each one alone; and one of them writes a final answer from the others' answers and reasoning excerpts. The first finding is about the benchmarks, not the models. Every problem on which all seven models were scored wrong was re-solved by a model, eight of them by hand as well: of the 48 items the audit touched, 21 had a wrong gold label and were relabelled, 22 were ill-posed and excluded, 2 were failures of our own grader, and on 3 the models were wrong and the gold stands. After the audit the seven families fail together on 1 problem of the 733 on which all seven finished an answer. That denominator hides the hard items: two of the models hit an 8,192-token cap on a third of GPQA Diamond and half of the olympiad problems, and when a cut-off answer is a missing vote rather than a reason to drop the item, every answering model is wrong on 20 problems of 987, 2%, 18 of them not yet audited. The second finding is about counting. The first two versions of this note scored a cut-off answer as a wrong vote in the debate and judge stages; this version scores it as absent, keeps the old tables in a sub-section for comparison, and most of what debate appeared to add goes with the change. On the answer side every operator lands at or below the best single model: plurality over the seven families is 0.8–1.9 points below the best member on three layers and ties it on the fourth; the seven are 2.1–2.3 effective voters. Debate across families gains 4.9 points of majority accuracy over the round-0 vote, but ten of its eleven fixes are on problems where an agent had been cut off before answering; on the 162 problems where all six agents answered it gains one item, and against the best agent on the items that agent answered it gains nothing on any layer. It takes unanimity from 152 to 219 of 225 items and the panel from 2.2 effective voters to 1.2, and it manufactures wrong unanimity: 0 unanimously wrong items at round 0, 4 after round 3, all four on problems the audit had not reached. Six copies of one model gain 3.5 points, half of which come from re-reading one's own answer with no peers at all. Role prompts leave the error correlation where temperature put it, within what 13–59 items can resolve. A synthesiser recovers 0 of 4 all-wrong problems. On the verifier side the picture is mixed. Asked to pick the correct candidate from the solvers' disagreeing answers on 77 contested problems, five judges whose errors correlate at φ = 0.16 reach 0.842 against 0.803 for the solvers' plurality under the same tie rule, 0.763 against 0.750 when a tie counts as wrong, and 0.946 on the 56 problems where more than half of them agree; the lead is 8 items against 5. Over the whole item set, plurality plus panel lands half a point below or a point above the best single solver, depending on the tie rule. Disagreement with five other families predicts a model's error at AUROC 0.98; disagreement among fifteen of its own resamples, 0.94. Asked instead to certify a single candidate, the same judges accept their own wrong answers 36–100% of the time, and a unanimous committee reaches a 2% false-acceptance rate only by rejecting 30% of the correct candidates. The swarm is a comparator and a warning light, not a second brain; on these benchmarks a good part of what it appeared to add was a second chance for an agent that had run out of tokens. Decomposition into subtasks, which is what industrial agent systems mostly do, is a different operator and is not measured here.
1

Where the premises come from

Nothing in the framing of this note is new; the measurements are. The premises come from four places, and it is worth naming them because the vocabulary of the LLM-swarm literature borrows from all four without always saying so.

Juries. Condorcet's jury theorem (1785) says that a majority of independent voters, each right with probability above one half, is right with probability that goes to one as the jury grows. Boland (1989) and Ladha (1992) worked out what happens when the votes are correlated: the majority's accuracy no longer goes to one but to a plateau set by the correlation, and above a threshold correlation adding voters adds nothing. The theorem is about a binary choice; its extension to a plurality over many candidate answers is List and Goodin (2001). Every "N agents beat one" claim is a Condorcet claim, and every vote ceiling in this note is a Boland–Ladha plateau; the coverage ceiling of Section 4 is a different bound.

Surveys. Kish (1965) gave the design effect for a cluster sample: n correlated observations with intraclass correlation ρ carry the information of n/[1+(n−1)ρ] independent ones, which is bounded by 1/ρ however large n grows. The first note on this site applied this to LLM resamples as the effective number of independent voters, neff. It is a formula about the variance of a mean; Section 8 shows what it does and does not say about a majority of binary votes.

Latent correlation. Two agents' correctness is a 2×2 table, and the Pearson correlation of two 0/1 variables, φ, cannot reach 1 unless their accuracies are equal. Pearson (1900) defined the tetrachoric correlation as the correlation of two latent normal variables that would produce the table, and gave the cos-π approximation to it; Fisher's intraclass correlation (1925), in the one-way analysis-of-variance form of Shrout and Fleiss (1979), is the panel version of φ on the observed scale, not a latent quantity; it is used here because the debate note used it. The one-factor Gaussian model used in Section 7 is Vasicek's (1987) single-factor model of correlated defaults, in which every borrower loads on one latent economic state; here every model would load on one latent item difficulty.

LLM ensembles. Self-consistency, the plurality over sampled chains of thought, is Wang et al. (2022, arXiv:2203.11171). Multi-agent debate in the form used here, agents that read each other's answers and revise, is Du et al. (2023, arXiv:2305.14325). Mixture-of-agents, a model that reads several answers and writes one, is Wang et al. (2024, arXiv:2406.04692). The LLM-as-a-judge paradigm and its self-preference bias are Zheng et al. (2023, arXiv:2306.05685) and Panickssery et al. (2024, arXiv:2404.13076). The 2025–26 papers whose claims this corpus was built to test are named in Section 17; three of them frame the whole note: the co-failure ceiling (arXiv:2606.27288), which bounds routing, voting and any scheme that returns one member's answer by the rate at which all members fail together; the modal and correlation ceilings of test-time scaling (arXiv:2606.28661), which the previous note tested on the public debate traces of arXiv:2609.35279 (the DebateLedger release); and Nine Judges, Two Effective Votes (arXiv:2605.29800), which found a panel of nine judges to be about two effective votes; Section 13 measures the same quantity for five judges on a different task and finds three.

The three earlier notes on this site1 established, on public data, that LLM pools are one to two effective voters, that diversity metrics do not predict vote gain once accuracy is controlled, and that debate raises correlation while escaping unanimous error on weak models. This note puts all of that on one corpus with the same items for every operator. It also found, on the way, that the co-failure rate those premises are built around cannot be measured on these benchmarks without first auditing the benchmarks.

The axes

The hypotheses in the swarm literature can be sorted into eight questions; this is one such sorting, fixed as axes before the corpus was built, and it is a working taxonomy rather than a claim about the field. The note covers five of them.

A. Parallel answers and aggregation
Does pooling independent answers add intelligence? Sections 7–8.
B. Debate
Is a multi-round exchange a different operator from sampling, and what does it transmit? Sections 10–11, with synthesis in Section 12.
C. Selection and verification
Can a verifier reach the ceiling that voting cannot, and how independent is it from the solvers? Sections 13–15.
D. Sources of diversity
Can independence be bought with families, roles, prompts or temperature? Sections 7–9.
G. Cost at equal budget
What does each operator cost in tokens, and when does it pay against one model given the same budget? The token side is in Section 16; the cost-matched comparison is the next note.

Three axes are not in this note. E, iterated chains: swarms in time, self-training and iterated in-context learning, and whether they converge to the prior. F, organisation with tools: agents that decompose a task, take roles, keep memory and call code or search, which is what industrial agent systems do; the corpus has no decomposition and no tools. H, theory as bound: which of the classical results above is tight on which data; the Kish check in Section 8 is a first instalment, not a treatment.

3

Why one corpus

A literature-based-discovery pipeline over about a thousand agent-swarm papers paired one paper's stated limitation with another paper's result wherever the two did not cite each other, scored each pair with an LLM judge, and produced 947 candidate hypotheses with a score of 4 or more out of 10, 140 of them at 6 or more. A handful were tested the usual way, one bridge at a time, two of them in the previous notes. Reading the rest made the economics obvious: most ask for one of five or six measurements on one kind of data, a correctness matrix across model families, resamples of one model, multi-round debate traces with every intermediate answer kept, a judge panel, and the token count of each call. A bridge test that costs ten dollars is not worth running; a corpus that answers forty bridges for fifty dollars is.

So the corpus was designed from the hypotheses, built in two days, and each of the 140 was then read against its tables by a model instructed to say "supported", "not supported", "mixed" or "the corpus does not measure this". After three passes, each adding the cuts the previous pass had asked for, 27 hypotheses had an answer and 113 asked for things the corpus does not contain. Section 17 re-reads sixteen of the 27 against the audited tables. The rest of this note is the measurements themselves, with the definitions first because half the disagreements in this literature are about what the words mean.

4

Terms

Item, layer, correct
An item is one problem with one gold answer. A layer is a source dataset. A model's answer is correct or not. A truncated answer (the model hit its completion cap, 8,192 tokens for the OpenRouter models, before writing a final line) is missing: it is not a vote, not a dissenter, not a row in a correlation, and an item is not dropped because of it unless the table says so. Where a single agent is the control, its truncated answer scores 0, because a model that does not answer has not answered. The first two versions of this note counted truncated rows as wrong in the debate and judge stages; Section 10 keeps those tables in a sub-section.
Answer identity
Two answers are the same answer when they are equal after normalisation: numbers as numbers to seven significant digits, the grader's own tolerance (24, 24.00, $24, 5,072,530 and 5072529.6 are one answer each), LaTeX fractions and roots in one form. Every plurality, unanimity and candidate count in this note uses this identity. The first version compared raw strings; the second rounded to six decimals, which still split a handful of large GSM-Hard answers by formatting and so counted six items as contested that were not.
Best single
The highest accuracy among the agents on the same items, a truncated answer scoring 0. The standard control in the ensemble literature. It is the floor of the box: any aggregation that lands below it has cost accuracy. Where it matters, the note also gives the best agent's accuracy on the items that agent answered, which is the fairer control for a capped model and the one that decides whether debate adds information rather than tokens. Both are in-sample maxima over six or seven agents and flatter the single model by a point or so on the small layers.
Plurality
The answer given by the largest number of agents, ties broken by first occurrence, as in self-consistency. "Majority" is used for the same thing when the agents are a debate panel. Plurality is computed on answer identity, so two agents who are both wrong in different ways do not vote together.
Ceiling
The share of items on which at least one agent is correct, 1 − P(all wrong); the co-failure ceiling 1 − β of arXiv:2606.27288, the "oracle" or "coverage" of the test-time-scaling literature. No scheme that chooses among the agents' answers can exceed it; a scheme that writes a new answer can. The box between best single and ceiling is where every answer-side result in this note lives. After the label audit the ceiling is 0.998–1.000 on the items all seven models finished and 0.97–0.99 when a truncated answer is a missing vote (Section 7).
Unanimous, unanimous-and-wrong
All agents gave the same answer; that answer is wrong. A unanimously wrong item is invisible to any rule that uses agreement as a confidence signal. Before the audit, every unanimously wrong matrix item turned out to have a wrong or ambiguous gold label.
Fixed, broken
In a debate, an item whose plurality was wrong at round 0 and correct at the last round, and the reverse; arXiv:2609.35279 calls them corrections and collapses.
φ
The Pearson correlation between two agents' 0/1 correctness vectors over items, averaged over the pairs on which it is defined (the phi coefficient, Yule 1912). Each pair is computed on the items both agents answered. It is exact but bounded away from 1 when the two accuracies differ, undefined when either agent is constant, and it understates the correlation of the latent errors.
Tetrachoric ρ
The correlation of two latent normal variables that would produce the observed 2×2 table of correct/incorrect. Pearson (1900); we use his cos-π approximation, which is poor when the margins are skewed and degenerates to ±1 when a cell of the table is empty. Pairs with an empty cell are dropped from the average, and the number of pairs that survive is printed next to it; after the audit few survive on the small layers.
neff
Kish's (1965) effective sample size, n/[1+(n−1)φ], as used in Effective Independence. A design-effect formula from survey sampling, used here as a descriptive summary of how much independence n agents bought; Section 8 compares the majority it predicts with the majority observed.
ICC
Fisher's intraclass correlation in the one-way ANOVA form ICC(1) of Shrout and Fleiss (1979), of correctness within an item, the share of the variance of "this agent was correct on this item" that is explained by the item. An observed-scale quantity, close to φ on the debate panel and below it on the matrix; reported because the debate note used it.
Contested item
Introduced here. For the judge panel: the candidate answers disagree by answer identity and at least one of them is correct. On a contested item a perfect selector scores 1 and the plurality scores less; it is the only kind of item on which a verifier can earn anything.
Pick vs certify
Introduced here as names; both tasks exist in the LLM-as-a-judge literature as pairwise/listwise and pointwise judging. Pick: see all distinct candidates with a reasoning excerpt each, choose one or say "none". Certify: see one candidate alone, say correct or incorrect.
AUROC
Area under the receiver operating characteristic curve: the probability that a randomly chosen error receives a higher warning score than a randomly chosen correct answer; 0.5 is no information, 1 is perfect.

Method

Design. The unit of analysis is an item answered by several agents, and every stage of the corpus is a different way of producing several answers to the same item: by different models, by resampling one model, by rounds of debate, by role prompts, by judging. The stages were chosen from the list of measurements the 140 hypotheses asked for, in descending order of how many hypotheses each would serve, and each was given a spend cap before it ran. Nothing was added after seeing a result except the cuts described below.

Controls. For every operator the comparison is to the best single agent on the same items and to the ceiling on the same items; both are computed from the same rows, never from a separate run. Debate has two controls built in: the round-0 plurality, which is what voting would have done, and the self-only arm, in which copies revise without seeing peers, which is what re-elicitation alone does. Judges are compared to the solvers' plurality on the same contested items, to each other, and, over the whole item set, to the best single solver. All comparisons are within-item; no number in this note compares one set of items against another.

Statistics. Accuracies are plain proportions. φ is the mean pairwise Pearson correlation of 0/1 correctness; the tetrachoric estimate is the cos-π approximation; ICC is the one-way ANOVA estimator; neff is Kish's formula applied to φ. The Kish-predicted majority is the probability that a majority of neff independent votes at the mean accuracy is correct: the exact binomial majority, ties split, at the two integers around neff, interpolated linearly. The first version of this note rounded neff to an integer, which made the prediction p² whenever it rounded to 2; the second used a normal approximation, which at n < 3 is off by up to four points in a direction set by p, not by φ, and produced a pattern Section 8 of that version mistook for a finding. Intervals, where given, are Wilson 95% intervals on proportions, or percentile bootstrap intervals over items (2,000 resamples) for differences; paired comparisons are exact McNemar tests on discordant items, uncorrected for the four layers. The strata of interest are small enough that the reader can see the counts, and they are reported as counts. Spearman correlations over model triples are over 35 triples per layer, which share models and are not independent.

Labels. After the first version of this note was written, the items on which the models had all been scored wrong were re-solved by Claude Opus 5.5 with explicit arithmetic, eight of them re-solved by hand as a check, the three GPQA Diamond items by hand only. The audit file holds 48 items. Forty-one of them are the items on which all seven models had finished an answer and been scored wrong; six more are items on which every debate agent that finished was wrong while one or two agents had been cut off; one, a GSM-Hard item with a negative count of toys, was added on inspection although four models had matched its gold. The decisions: 21 gold labels were wrong and were relabelled with the demonstrably correct answer (9 MMLU-Pro, 11 GSM-Hard, 1 GPQA Diamond); 22 items were ill-posed or had two defensible options and are excluded from every table (15 MMLU-Pro, 6 GSM-Hard, 1 GPQA Diamond); on 3 the models were wrong and the gold stands; and 2 olympiad items had been scored wrong because the matrix stage had graded expressions with the numeric grader (1/576 against \frac{1}{576}). Every row of every stage was re-graded with the normalising grader, which changed 61 further rows in the matrix, all on HMMT expressions. The first revision reported these as 45 items in a 24/16/2/2 split; that arithmetic did not match the file and is withdrawn. An independent re-check of the file for this revision agreed with all 11 GSM-Hard relabels and 20 of the 21 overall, and would have excluded rather than relabelled one MMLU-Pro item whose two options say the same thing. The audit, with the reason for each decision, is label-audit.json in the companion repository, and the re-grading script makes no model calls. Three things the audit does not cover. Items on which some model matched a wrong gold label were not read, so individual accuracies may still carry label noise in both directions. Items on which every answering model was wrong while another model had been cut off by its token cap were not selected, because the selection rule asked for seven finished answers; there are 18 such items after the audit, plus one all-seven-wrong MMLU-Pro item that sits in an older run file outside the tables, and they are listed in the audit file as unaudited. And the four items on which the six-agent debate is unanimously wrong at round 3 meet the stated rule but were not read either; two of them are GSM-Hard items whose gold has the hallmarks of partial substitution. All of these stay unrelabelled in this version and the note says so where they matter. The auditor is one of the audited models, and the new gold agrees with Opus's own first answer on every relabel; a reader who distrusts that should start from the file.

Truncation. The four OpenRouter models ran under an 8,192-token completion cap; the Claude models, through the CLI, had none. GLM and Qwen hit the cap on 68 and 19 of 573 MMLU-Pro items, 76 and 44 of 197 GPQA Diamond, 8 and 8 of 144 GSM-Hard and 42 and 50 of 90 olympiad items at temperature 0, and on 86 of the 1,350 round-0 rows of the debate; DeepSeek on 2. The matrix tables of Section 7 use the items every model finished, which drops the hard items and is said so there. In every other stage a truncated row is absent: it does not vote, it is not a dissenter, it is not in φ, ICC or the unanimity count, and an agent's accuracy is over the rows it answered; where a single agent is the control, its truncated answer scores 0. The first two versions of this note treated a truncated row as a wrong answer in the debate and judge stages, which depressed round-0 accuracy and correlation on exactly the layers where the capped models truncate, and produced the debate gains those versions reported; Section 10 keeps the old tables in a sub-section so that the two policies can be read side by side. Items are included in a stage's table when every agent of that stage has a row for it, truncated or not, which is why item counts differ between tables and are printed in each.

Exclusions. The 22 audited items are dropped everywhere. Five of the first-pass layers (MATH-500, GSM8K, CommonsenseQA, StrategyQA, Knights & Knaves) were dropped before any aggregation was computed. The first two versions said the criterion was that every model exceeded 0.95 on them; the matrix file does not bear that out (CommonsenseQA is 0.80–0.93 for the seven, Mistral is 0.66 on Knights & Knaves), and the honest description is that they were dropped as knowledge or near-saturated layers by judgment, which is a researcher degree of freedom. They remain in the matrix file. Nine of the 240 debates were never run because Mistral had no matrix answer to seed them with, and one debate lost Mistral on a rate limit; the six-agent tables use the 225 items with a row for every agent in every round, and a five-agent recount without Mistral is in the companion tables.

Post-hoc cuts. The first reading of the hypotheses against the tables asked for cuts the first tables did not have: the all-wrong stratum of debate, the transition table by round-0 correct count, the flip table, the copula and two-state fits, the judge committee thresholds, the error-detection AUROC. These were computed from the existing rows, with no new calls. The three homogeneous-debate arms, the certify stage and the synthesis stage were new runs made after the first reading, each with its own cap; they are the only data collected after a result had been seen. The label audit, the tie analysis of the judge panel and the whole-set accounting of Section 13 were added in the second version, after an external reading of the first, with no new calls. This third version, after a second external reading, changed the truncation policy, merged candidate answers within the grader's tolerance, replaced the Kish estimator, re-counted the audit, reported judge coverage, and replaced the reversible GPQA answer hashes in the companion data; again no new calls, and the old debate and judge tables are kept for comparison.

Reading the hypotheses. The 140 hypotheses were read against the first-version tables by Claude Opus 5.5 at low effort, three times, with instructions to quote only numbers present in the tables and to return "insufficient" whenever the quantity was not measured. The verdicts in Section 17 are the author's re-reading of those sixteen hypotheses against the audited tables.

The corpus

Items. 240 problems with a single checkable answer, sixty from each of four layers: MMLU-Pro (ten-option multiple choice, stratified over subjects), GPQA Diamond (four-option graduate science), GSM-Hard (GSM8K word problems with large numbers substituted by a program; Section 5 says what that did to its gold labels), and the 2024 and 2025 AIME (integer answers), with the February 2025 HMMT (short expressions) in the matrix only; all public before the models' training cutoffs, so contamination is possible and unmeasured. A first pass over 1,576 items had included MATH-500, GSM8K, CommonsenseQA, StrategyQA and Knights & Knaves puzzles; they were dropped from every table (Section 5 says on what grounds) and remain in the matrix file. The seven-family matrix covers 573 MMLU-Pro items, 197 GPQA Diamond, 144 GSM-Hard and 90 olympiad after the exclusions; the debate, sampling and judging stages use the sixty per layer, and their sixty olympiad items are the AIME ones.

Models. Seven families, all current reasoning models in October 2026 and all allowed to think before answering, though not equally: Claude Opus 5.5, Sonnet 5.5 and Haiku 4.5 through the Claude Code CLI, where temperature is not controllable, the effort setting was low and there is no completion cap, so that Opus and Sonnet averaged 54 and 19 reasoning tokens per MMLU-Pro answer while Haiku averaged 2,200 and ran to 25,000 on the olympiad; and DeepSeek V3.2, GLM 5.3 Flash, Qwen 3.7 Plus and Mistral Large 2512 through OpenRouter under an 8,192-token completion cap, with the serving provider logged per call and no separate reasoning-token count returned. GLM and Qwen hit the cap on a third of GPQA Diamond and half of the olympiad problems (Section 5). Four further models answered subsets of the matrix and are not used here. Opus is in the matrix but not in the debate or judge panels; the panel was fixed at six agents when the stage was configured, and the design notes do not record why Opus was the one left out, so the reader should treat its absence as unexplained rather than principled.

Grading. Letters by exact match; numbers by numeric comparison with a relative tolerance of 10−6; expressions by a normaliser (fractions, roots, dollar signs, trailing approximations) plus an LLM arbiter (Opus, decisions cached) where the normaliser and the gold string still disagreed. Every answer was extracted from a required last line, FINAL: <answer>. The first version of this note had graded the matrix stage with the numeric grader alone, which is the grading failure described in Section 5.

Voting across families

The oldest question first: does the plurality of seven different models beat the best of them? On the items all seven finished, on no layer does it. The last column was the surprise of the second version, and it needs the same caveat. On the items all seven finished, the share on which someone was right is 0.998 on MMLU-Pro and 1.000 on the other three layers: of 733 items, the seven models all fail on one. The first version reported all-wrong rates of 1–10% and fitted a one-factor latent model to them; those rates were made of wrong gold labels, ill-posed questions and a grader that could not read a fraction. But the items the capped models could not finish are the hard ones. Count a cut-off answer as a missing vote and keep every item that at least five models answered, and every answering model is wrong on 12 of 566 MMLU-Pro items, 2 of 191 GPQA Diamond, 4 of 143 GSM-Hard and 2 of 87 olympiad: 20 of 987, 2.0%, ten times the headline rate. The last column is the third estimator this note has tried for the same quantity, and the lesson is about the estimator. Kish's neff was used in the earlier notes as a ceiling on what a vote could achieve; the first version of this note called it a floor, on an estimator that rounded neff to 2 and so predicted p²; the second version used a normal approximation and reported that the Kish majority over-predicted the plurality where φ was high and under-predicted it where φ was low. With the exact binomial, a majority of 1.3–2.1 independent votes is the single-draw accuracy for neff ≤ 2 and barely more above it, so Kish predicts that resampling buys nothing, and the observed plurality is above that prediction on six of eight rows, by one to fourteen points, most where φ is lowest. A design-effect formula built for the variance of a survey mean does not predict a plurality of correlated binary votes at these neff; it is a descriptive summary of how much independence the draws bought, and the two hypotheses in the literature that bet on its direction get no verdict from it (Section 17).

Synthesis

The generative objection to everything above is that a vote is the wrong aggregator: a model that reads six answers can write a seventh, and the mixture-of-agents literature reports exactly that. We gave DeepSeek, at temperature 0, the six round-3 answers of the mixed debate, each with the last 900 characters of that agent's reasoning, and the instruction to pick one or derive a different answer if all were wrong; separately, five of its own draws with their reasoning tails. The first version of this note called these inputs "transcripts"; they are final answers with excerpts, about 1,400 input tokens per item.

Two caveats on the design. The debate-answer test is weak as a test of mixture-of-agents, because by round 3 the answering agents agree on 219 of 225 items and there is little to mix; the own-draws test, which does start from diverse inputs, is the fairer one, and it says the same thing. On these models and these problems, reading the others' reasoning is not a way to get a new answer; it is a way to pick among the old ones.

Judges, picking

Everything so far asks the group to produce the answer, and the box says the prize is in selection: on a contested item some model is right and the plurality may not be, and a verifier that finds the right one collects the distance between plurality and ceiling, which on the sixty-item subsets is 3–11 points at round 0. The earlier notes predicted that an LLM verifier cannot, because its errors are the solvers' errors. Part of that prediction holds; the part that fails is the useful part, and it is smaller than the first two versions of this note made it.

On the 77 items where the six debate agents' matrix answers disagreed by answer identity and at least one was correct, each of five models was shown the distinct candidates, each with a 700-character excerpt of one proposer's reasoning, in a shuffled anonymised order, and asked to pick one or say none. That is the whole of the comparator result: comparing candidates is a task on which these models' errors are not more correlated than when producing them, the gain on contested items is 8 items against 5, and it is at most the size of the gap between a vote and its best voter.

Two things qualify this further. On the 2 items where no candidate was correct, the judges said "none" once in 10 opportunities; a picker cannot abstain. And the result rests on 76 contested items from one corpus, 13 of them decided by a tie-break and 44 judged by a panel of fewer than five. It is the only positive number in this note, it is within noise, and it is the one that most needs a second dataset.

Peers as an error detector

The same independence shows up in a cheaper form. Take DeepSeek's matrix answer on each of the 225 debate items, wrong on 17% of them, and ask how well two warning signals rank the wrong ones: the number of the other five families whose answer differs from it, and the number of DeepSeek's own temperature-1 draws that differ from it, the usual self-consistency signal. Peer disagreement, counted over the peers that answered and by answer identity, reaches AUROC 0.98; disagreement among three own draws, 0.89, among five, 0.91, among fifteen, 0.94. The comparison is not quite between "others" and "self": one strong peer alone, Sonnet, reaches 0.93, so part of the peer signal is that the peers are better solvers. What survives the matching is a gap of four points between five one-shot calls to other families and fifteen extra calls to oneself, with a bootstrap interval over items of [+0.01, +0.08]; the gap between the one strong peer and fifteen own draws is −0.01 and its interval spans zero. Five cheap calls to other families are at least as good an abstention signal as fifteen to oneself, for DeepSeek, the only target tested. This is the mechanism behind the panel in Section 13: a judge's "this candidate is right" is partly "this candidate agrees with what I would have said", and that signal is less correlated across families than the answers themselves.

Two things stand out. Sonnet is a good certifier, 6% false acceptance and one decoy in 65; the rest accept a quarter to a half of wrong candidates, and a planted wrong answer that no proposer had offered is accepted 12–26% of the time by the other four. And the dominant term is self-preference: every judge accepts its own wrong answer more often than someone else's, by a factor of 1.1 for DeepSeek, 2.8 for Haiku, 3.4 for GLM and Qwen, and 6.6 for Sonnet, on 5–35 own wrong answers each. The candidate was anonymised and shown with another proposer's excerpt when several models gave it, so this is not style recognition of the excerpt; whether it is recognition of the answer or re-derivation of the same error this corpus cannot say (Section 18), and "own" here means "equals the judge's own matrix answer", which is consistency rather than recognition.

A committee repairs this only at a price. The false acceptances correlate at φ = 0.38 across the five judges, 2.0 effective judges, between the solver number and the picking number. Over the 94 candidates all five ruled on (48 of them GSM-Hard, 20 of the 54 wrong ones planted decoys):

Put next to Section 13: the same five models, the same items, nearly the same candidates, and in one framing they are 3.0 judges whose strict majority is right 95% of the time where it exists, in the other they are 2.0 correlated solvers who wave through their own mistakes unless all five are asked. The difference is whether the judge compares or re-solves. The swarm can rank, a little; it can certify only when unanimous and at a steep recall cost; and it cannot abstain.

What the ledger says

On the answer, multiplying agents moves accuracy inside a box whose floor is the best member and whose ceiling is the share of problems someone got right. After auditing the benchmarks, that ceiling is 0.998–1.000 on the 733 problems all seven models finished, and the "co-failure" the first version of this note measured there was the benchmarks failing, not the models; on the problems two of the models could not finish under their token cap it is about 0.98, unaudited. Inside the box, voting across families lands below the floor unless the strongest model is missing from the vote. Resampling, debate, roles and synthesis land between the walls. Debate is a procedure for a correct minority to win and for a cut-off agent to answer: ten of its eleven fixes are on problems where an agent had run out of tokens, against the best agent on the problems it answered it gains nothing, it stops being decorrelated within two rounds, and it ends unanimously wrong on four problems whose labels the audit has not reached. On verification the picture is mixed: asked to compare candidates, five models are 3.0 effective judges, their strict majority is right 95% of the time where it exists, and on top of a vote they bring a pool to within a point of its best member on either side; asked to certify one candidate, they are 2.0 correlated solvers with a self-preference term on top, and only their unanimity is trustworthy.

Five things this does not say. It does not say that a swarm of agents working on different subtasks, with tools, memory and a plan, adds nothing; that is a different operator, its gains would come from context length and parallel time rather than from independent errors, and the right control for it, one agent doing the same decomposition sequentially at the same token budget, is not in this corpus. It does not say anything about base or non-reasoning models, where error correlation may be lower and voting may pay; every model here was allowed to think, though the Claude models at low effort barely did, and the previous note's weak models behaved differently. It does not say debate is useless: a 4.9-point gain over the round-0 vote with a stopping rule that costs a seventh of three rounds is a real engineering result, and the fact that most of it is a capped model finishing its answer is itself a result about what debate does. It does not say the comparator result is large or general: it is one to three points on 235 problems from one corpus, a tie-break away from zero on the contested ones, and within noise. And it does not say that strong models never fail together: it says that on four public benchmarks the cases where they appeared to were, about four times in five, the benchmark's fault on the problems the audit read, that twenty more such problems sit behind a token cap and have not been read, and that any measurement of joint failure on such data has to start with an audit and a budget the hard problems fit in.

What this settles in the literature

The hypotheses the corpus was built for are bridges written by the discovery pipeline: each pairs a stated limitation of one paper with a result of another, and the prediction is the pipeline's, not either paper's. They were judged before any of this data existed, and the companion repository of the discovery pipeline holds all 140 with their three verdict passes against the first-version tables. The sixteen the tables answer, re-read by the author against the audited tables, by each hypothesis's own falsification criterion:

  • Correlated judge panels (a bridge from CollabEval, arXiv:2603.00993, to Nine Judges, Two Effective Votes, arXiv:2605.29800). Predicted: discussion rounds raise φ and cut neff, and items that reach consensus after discussion are wrong more often than items unanimous before it. Supported: φ 0.34 → 0.85, neff 2.2 → 1.2, unanimous-and-wrong 0 of 152 → 4 of 219 (Section 10). Two caveats: these are solver debates, not judge panels, and the four items are not audited.
  • Pre-debate independence predicts debate gain (OptAgent, arXiv:2510.18032, read against Effective Independence). Predicted: gain over the best single agent tracks the panel's pre-debate neff, and debate drives neff toward 1 whatever the topology. Not separable on this corpus. The two layers with low round-0 neff (2.8–2.9) are the two on which the capped models were cut off most; against the best agent that finished every item debate gains there (+8.9, +3.7) and loses on the high-φ layers, which is what the second version reported, but against the best agent on the items it answered it gains +0.3 and 0, and on the 162 items with six answers it gains one item (Section 10). That neff falls toward 1 is supported in every arm, and the self-only control of Section 11 shows that seeing peers is what drives it, for copies of one model. Four layers is four data points.
  • Relay gains live inside the selection ceiling (WILC, arXiv:2607.29087, against the co-failure ceiling paper, arXiv:2606.27288). Predicted: iterative collaboration gains almost entirely on items where some worker was already right. Supported, with a smaller denominator than before: the gain sits in the one- and two-correct strata, mostly on items where a worker had been cut off; the all-wrong stratum has one item after the audit, and debate rescued it.
  • Mixture-of-agents synthesis beats the ceiling (a bridge from arXiv:2605.01566 to arXiv:2606.27288). Predicted: an aggregator's surplus over voting comes from items where every proposer was wrong. Not supported: 0 of 16 and 0 of 4 (Section 12); the surplus is selection inside the ceiling.
  • Role prompts decorrelate (Dynamic Role Assignment, arXiv:2601.17152, against the Blackwell-informativeness paper, arXiv:2605.06028). Predicted: role-diversified proposals have lower inter-agent error correlation than temperature samples. Not supported, and not refuted: φ is higher under roles on every layer, by 0.06–0.22, with bootstrap intervals that include zero (Section 9). A sibling hypothesis from the same bridge, that a reported debate gain is score-only selection and the subsequent debate erases diversity (against The Interaction Tax, arXiv:2608.23541), is also not supported: debate raised accuracy above the round-0 vote, and selection by a single judge did not beat the vote.
  • Kish over-predicts the vote (two hypotheses pairing Kish 1965 with Boland 1989 and with arXiv:2607.23931). Predicted: neff = 1/φ overstates what a majority can reach. No verdict. With an exact estimator the Kish majority at neff ≤ 2 is the single-draw accuracy, which the observed plurality exceeds on six of eight rows; the hypothesis assumed the formula would over-state a vote, and on these rows it predicts no vote at all (Section 8). The first version reported the reverse of the hypothesis on an estimator that rounded neff to an integer, and the second a mixed verdict on a normal approximation whose error tracked accuracy rather than φ; both readings are withdrawn. The companion hypothesis from arXiv:2602.08003, that a probit one-factor bound beats the Kish prediction, has no table behind it in this corpus and gets no verdict either.
  • A one-factor model under-predicts joint failure by 2–3× (a bridge from the Honest Quorum paper, arXiv:2607.16109, to Effective Independence; the 2–3× figure is the co-failure paper's 2.5×, arXiv:2606.27288). Insufficient after the audit: on the items all seven finished the observed all-wrong rate is one in 733 and the copula at the tetrachoric ρ over-predicts it; on the items with cut-off answers it is about 2% and unaudited (Section 7). There is no measured joint-failure tail on these benchmarks to compare a model against. The same bridge's claim that provider-family diversity does not make a certifying committee feasible is supported in part by Section 15: false endorsements correlate at 0.38, and only unanimity reaches a 5% bound, at 70% recall.
  • Pooled independence ≲ 2 (CTTS, arXiv:2508.03333, against No Free Lunch). Predicted: a heterogeneous pool has about two effective members and selection stays within the union of candidates. Supported in part: neff 2.1–2.3 of 7 on the three larger layers, every aggregator inside the ceiling; the paper's own method was not run.
  • Debate rounds add nothing beyond round-0 voting (TUMIX, arXiv:2510.01279, against the modal-ceiling paper, arXiv:2606.28661), the hypothesis the previous note tested on public traces. Not supported on the full item set, on a second dataset with different models: majority 0.924 → 0.973, 11 fixed against 0 broken. Supported on the 162 items on which every agent had answered at round 0, where the gain is one item. The verdict turns on whether a round that lets a cut-off agent answer counts as refinement. (TUMIX's own abstract reports gains from refinement; the "nothing" is the bridge's reading of its halting result.)
  • Majority accuracy peaks before consensus (the Koopman-certification paper, arXiv:2608.05956, against Ladha 1992). Not supported: majority accuracy is non-decreasing to near-unanimity, with no interior peak (Section 10), within the resolution of four rounds.
  • Debate gains when errors are already correlated (arXiv:2511.07784 against arXiv:2605.29800). Not supported, reversed in the per-layer table: the gain over the round-0 vote is largest where φ starts lowest, which is in line with that paper's own abstract. The reversal is not clean, because the low-φ layers are the layers with cut-off answers.
  • Prompted deference does not control convergence (Thinking with Many Minds, arXiv:2501.02348, against BOUNDARY_SYNC, arXiv:2607.01600). Supported with two prompt levels: exposure drives convergence, the stated weight barely moves it (Section 11).

Each of these is one reading of one table, and each hypothesis was written about a specific system this corpus does not reproduce. They are listed as what a careful reader of these tables would conclude about those claims, not as replications of the papers.

Limitations

  • Labels. The audit covered 48 items, selected mostly on all seven models having finished and failed; it found the gold wrong or the item ill-posed on 43 of them, by the author's reading, and on 36–42 by an independent re-reading, depending on how the judgment calls are counted. Items on which some model agreed with a wrong gold label have not been audited, so per-model accuracies may still be biased; 18 items on which every answering model was wrong while another model was cut off, one all-seven-wrong item in an older run file, and the four items on which the debate ends unanimously wrong have not been audited either, and are listed in the audit file. The auditor is one of the audited models and the new gold agrees with its own first answer on every relabel; eight items were checked by hand, the three GPQA Diamond items by hand only. GSM-Hard's gold is produced by a program that substitutes large numbers into GSM8K; eleven of its 150 matrix items had the substitution applied to some quantities and not others (six of them among the sixty), and six more are not well-posed after substitution (three among the sixty).
  • Truncation. The four OpenRouter models ran under an 8,192-token completion cap and two of them hit it on a third of GPQA Diamond and half of the olympiad problems. The matrix tables are on the items every model finished, which are the easier ones; the debate and judge tables count a cut-off row as absent; the first two versions counted it as wrong, and Section 10 shows what that changed. The cap is a property of this corpus, not of the models, and a re-run with a larger cap would move every number that depends on it.
  • Size. Sixty items per layer, 225–236 in the debate stages, 76 contested items for the picking result, 54 wrong candidates for the certifying one. The strata are small: the all-wrong stratum of debate is one item, six agents were alone against five answering dissenters six times, the panel result turns on 13 tied items and is 8 against 5 on the discordant ones, and the homogeneous arms differ by 11 fixes and 3 breaks against 8 and 4.
  • Models. Reasoning models of one vintage only, the Claude models at an uncontrolled temperature and low effort, so that two of the seven barely reasoned; the OpenRouter calls return no reasoning-token count, so "allowed to think" is a setting, not a measurement. The homogeneous debate, the personas, the synthesiser and the error-detection result rest on one family, DeepSeek V3.2. Opus is in the matrix but not in the debate or judge panels.
  • Controls. Best single is an in-sample maximum over six or seven agents, which flatters it by about a point on the small layers; on GPQA Diamond and GSM-Hard the one-item margins of Section 7 are inside that. Round 0 of the debate is the temperature-0 matrix answer and the rounds are at temperature 1, as in the public traces of the previous note; the self-only arm of Section 11 is the control for the share of the round-1 jump that is re-elicitation, about half for copies of DeepSeek, and there is no self-only arm for the mixed-family debate.
  • Mistral. Nine of the 240 debates were never run because Mistral had no matrix answer to seed them with, and one lost Mistral to a rate limit. The six-agent tables use the 225 complete items; the five-agent recount recovers one item and matches at round 3.
  • Estimators. Tetrachoric correlations are the cos-π approximation, averaged over the pairs on which it is defined, which after the audit is few on the small layers; φ and ICC are exact, φ on the items each pair answered. The copula is a simulation at one ρ per layer and is no longer a fit to anything. neff is Kish's formula used descriptively; the Kish majority is an exact binomial interpolated between integers, the third estimator this note has used for it. The debate stopping rule was evaluated on the items it was chosen on. Plurality ties go to the first agent in a fixed order; seven of the eleven debate fixes are round-0 ties.
  • Judging design. Candidates were anonymised but shown with a reasoning excerpt; a judge could recognise its own style. In the pick task a judge's own answer was among the candidates whenever it had solved the item; in the certify task "own" means the candidate equals the judge's matrix answer. The self-preference rates are therefore a mixture of style recognition, answer recognition and re-derivation, and this corpus cannot split them. The certify committee is 51% GSM-Hard candidates.
  • Coverage. In the pick task GLM and Qwen named a candidate on 48 and 57 of 77 items; in the certify task the OpenRouter judges ruled on 50–70% of the candidates Sonnet ruled on; the role runs did not reach GPQA; GLM's hard-layer samples are mostly truncated; Haiku's olympiad samples were drawn twice under the same indices and the later batch is the one counted.
  • Contamination. All four layers are public and predate the models; the olympiad problems are from 2024 and 2025 competitions. Nothing here measures memorisation.
  • Pre-registration. None. The stages were designed to serve hypotheses written by other people, and the verdict passes that read those hypotheses against the first tables were done by a model; both are in the discovery repository, not in this note. The first revision was prompted by an external reading of the first version, the second by an external reading of the second, and each found errors the author had not; the reader should assume a third reading would find more.

Reproduction

Every table in this note, and about twenty more that it quotes from, is rebuilt from the stored answers by a single standard-library script in a few seconds with no network access, under both truncation policies. The dataset and that script will be released as tech-note-5 within about a month of this note: every row of every stage with the parsed answer, its correctness, the serving provider and the token counts, but not the model texts; GPQA Diamond answers replaced by random per-option tokens, which preserve answer identity but not the option letter, and no question text or gold label stored, per that dataset's terms (the second version's hashes were an unsalted digest of the letter and could be inverted; they have been replaced). The release also carries label-audit.json, the item-by-item record of the audit with the reason for every relabel and exclusion and the lists of unaudited items, and regrade_all.py, the script that applied it, which imports the grader from the collection repository and will run once that is released. Figures are drawn inline from the same numbers. The collection scripts, graders, and the discovery pipeline that proposed the hypotheses and read the verdicts live in a separate repository that will accompany a later note on the pipeline itself. Until the release, the numbers here are the author's word; after it, they are a command.
