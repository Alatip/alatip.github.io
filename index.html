Decomposition Buys Budget at Best, Nothing Usually

Cut a problem into steps and let the same model solve them one at a time, at the cost of one whole attempt. What it could not solve whole it still cannot: on the hard numeric problems none is gained in eight tries. The gain lands only on the problems where a single pass is cut off by the token cap, and in dollars a split attempt costs three to nine whole draws, so the single model ties or wins at equal spend. Three other models solving the parts cost more and solve no more. Pre-registered, 560 runs, $5.73.

What a Model Orchestrator Can and Cannot Add

Sakana's orchestrators learn in training which model to trust and when to stop, and check nothing when they answer. That lifts them above the best model in their pool, and never above what the pool can do together. Where the Swarm Pays

One corpus of answer traces, seven reasoning models from five families, 240 hard problems, every cheap way of multiplying agents. The first finding is about the benchmarks: on the problems where all seven models were scored wrong, the gold label, the question or the grader was at fault on 45 of 48, and the "co-failure ceiling" on the problems the models finish is 0.998–1.000. The second is about counting: two of the models hit a token cap on the hard problems, and a cut-off answer scored as a wrong vote had manufactured most of debate's gain. Inside the box, plurality is below the best model on every layer; debate's gain is mostly a second chance for an agent that ran out of tokens, it gains one item on the problems every agent answered, and it ends unanimously wrong on four problems whose labels are unchecked; about half of a homogeneous debate's gain is re-reading one's own answer; personas add no measurable independence; synthesis recovers nothing. On verification the swarm pays a little: five judges picking among candidates lead the solvers' plurality by a margin that depends on the tie rule and is within noise, their strict majority is right 94% of the time where it exists, and peer disagreement flags errors at AUROC 0.98 against 0.93 for fifteen self-resamples. Asked to certify a single candidate they are solvers again, trustworthy only when unanimous. Revised three times the same day, after a label audit, a truncation audit and a reading of the code against the text, with every version kept and comparable; measured, not pre-registered; data and code released. code · doi

Debate Is Not Sampling

The modal ceiling of test-time scaling says plurality voting cannot beat the rate at which the most common answer is right. On 9,600 public debate traces, three-agent refinement beats the round-0 modal-hit rate on every weak model and corrects 31–45% of problems where all three agents started wrong; the exchangeability premise is what fails. The correlation ceiling does transfer: inter-agent correlation rises every round and the panel is one effective voter by round 3; stopping at unanimity beats the n_eff-plateau rule. Pre-registered, zero inference cost. code · doi

The Control Ate the Signal

Kim 2026 finds that diversity metrics barely predict majority-vote gain once accuracy is controlled for. On four public correctness matrices a heterogeneous Condorcet model with no dependence term predicts the same gain at Spearman 0.80 against 0.20 for the residualized metric; the tetrachoric dependence estimate adds 0.01. Gain over the best member is a nonlinear function of the accuracies that a linear control throws away. Pre-registered, zero inference cost. code · doi

There Is No Free Lunch After All

Notes before writing down a theorem. The boundary of what a model can solve is the closure of its training set under composition of bounded depth; swarms, chains, debate and short RLVR move mass inside it, only external data extends it. The claim, why it is nearly a tautology, where the content is, what threatens it, and the plan to test it. Nothing measured yet. doi
